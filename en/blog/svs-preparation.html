What a commander does before SvS

From the moment the enemy state is decided to the five-hour Castle Battle

September 27, 2026 CommandClock

I lead every SvS for my state as overall commander in a mobile base-war strategy game. I’ve done every role: solo reinforcing, swaps, and rally leader.

Here, step by step, is what I actually do as commander from the moment the enemy state is decided until the five-hour Castle Battle ends. Examples are from Whiteout Survival (unofficial).

First, the terms used in this article

Rally leader
The player who starts a rally.
Single rally
A rally by one player. When only the marked enemy is rallying, or when other enemies rally separately with time between them, each one is a single rally.
Chain attack
A single attack (rally) rarely breaks through, and when the enemy’s flag holder in the garrison is strong, you add more rallies to break it. That’s a chain attack. Two players make a chain of 2; three make a chain of 3.
Flag holder
The strongest player in the garrison. The player at the very front is sometimes also called the flag holder, so here we use it to mean the strongest player.
Swap
The attacking alliance hands a captured base to the occupying alliance, timed to the second. Players in the occupying alliance land solo.
Withdraw
During a swap, everyone garrisoned inside the base leaves at the same moment.
Reinforce
Sending troops in between the enemy’s chained landings to strengthen the defense.

The flow of SvS week

(00:00 UTC)		The enemy state is decided

1. Three things to do first once the enemy state is decided

1-1. Decide the war-phase rules and the seating areas

Coordinate with the enemy state to set the rules for the war phase, and share them within your own state.

Decide the seating areas at the same time: north, south, east, or west (up, down, left, right), and which side of the border line to use.

1-2. Ask for headcounts

With help from each alliance leader, ask for headcounts through in-alliance surveys and similar. You ask for the total, and for each alliance’s numbers so you can split players into the occupying alliance and the attacking alliances.

Also decide who will call people out in alliance chat.

1-3. Decide which enemies to mark

Whether to mark an enemy depends on how strong they are. If any chain-attack squad can break them, there’s no need to mark anyone in particular. Still, keep an eye on roughly the top 1–5 in the enemy state’s rankings.

2. What the commander actually does during the five live hours

2-1. Watch for enemy rallies from an account in the occupying alliance

Use an account kept on standby in the occupying alliance to watch for enemy rallies. When a marked enemy starts a rally, announce it on the voice call: “(enemy name) rally!”

When you don’t hold the base, you can’t see the timing of enemy rallies in the list. In that case, look down on the marked enemies’ cities from above and judge whether they’re rallying.

2-2. Change the next decision depending on a single rally or a chain

Within about 5–10 seconds before or after a marked enemy starts a rally, watch whether other enemies have lined up with it. If they have, it’s a chain attack; if not, it’s a single rally. The next decision changes depending on which it is.

2-3. Cut the enemy’s occupation time with an immediate rally

When an enemy starts a rally, act on the assumption that they will break your garrison.

The time from being broken until the attacking alliance breaks that enemy becomes the enemy’s occupation time. So when an enemy rallies, start a rally right away so you can break them. That way you cut the enemy’s occupation time.

2-4. On a single rally, decide whether to swap

On a single rally, decide whether to swap.

When enemy rallies spread out and a perfect window opens, decide whether to swap right away, or have the attacking alliance hold on and swap once things settle. When swapping, also call the time for the withdraw, when everyone in the garrison leaves at the same moment.

2-5. On a chain attack, call the reinforce time

When the enemy rallies in a chain, call the time to reinforce.

I do this for five hours while on a voice call.

3. What gets in the way most during the live battle

When an enemy rally starts, I give the landing time to the attacking alliance’s rally leaders. The delay comes from calculating the landing time and rounding the seconds to a clean number.

That delay before the call is, in the end, occupation time we let the enemy gain.

Everyone who gets the call also has to work out “landing time − my march time” one by one before pressing.
